# How the Mafia Actually Works: Research Report for Simulation Design

Research date: 2026-09-21. Sources: ~35 web searches (Wikipedia, Mob Museum, DOJ/FBI, SEC testimony, court records, Addiopizzo, Britannica, journalistic archives). Figures from cooperating witnesses (Gravano, Valachi, Franzese, Buscetta) are self-reported and should be read as order-of-magnitude.

## 1. Organizational structure

### The American "family" (borgata)
The hierarchy codified after 1931 and confirmed by every major cooperator (Valachi 1963, Fratianno, Gravano 1992, Massino 2005):

- **Boss (rappresentante / don)**: final authority; every murder of a member, every promotion, every new inductee requires his approval. He does not commit crimes directly; his income is tribute.
- **Underboss (sottocapo)**: day-to-day operator, successor-in-waiting, often runs the "administration" while the boss is jailed. In practice the underboss receives most of the tribute envelopes (Gravano did this for Gotti).
- **Consigliere**: counsellor and, in theory, elected by the members to represent soldiers to the boss and to arbitrate disputes. In practice a boss's trusted friend. Sits with boss and underboss as the "administration."
- **Caporegime / capo / captain / skipper**: commands a crew (regime, decina). Historically the Sicilian decina meant ~10 men; American crews range from roughly 6 to 30+ soldiers plus their associates. A capo's value to the boss is measured by the size and regularity of his tribute.
- **Soldier (soldato, "made man", "wiseguy", "friend of ours", "button")**: a sworn member. He is his own small business: he runs his rackets, keeps a share, pays his capo. He may not be killed or even struck without the boss's permission.
- **Associates ("friend of mine", "connected guy")**: everyone else who works with the family, from Jewish and Irish partners (Lansky, Burke) to gofers. They have no protection. Associates must be Italian (after the 1970s, in most families, full-Italian paternal descent) to be proposed for membership.

Family sizes: Valachi's 1963 testimony detailed a Genovese tree of 142 individuals; the FBI's 2013 estimates put the Genovese at ~250 made members and the Gambino at ~200; the Bonanno, Lucchese and Colombo were each ~100–150 at their late-20th-century strength. Each made man typically has 3–10 associates around him, so a family of 200 members may command 1,000–2,000 people. Chicago is one family, "the Outfit", divided into roughly six "street crews" each with a franchise over a territory.

### Promotion, "opening the books", and the rule of introductions
- A candidate is "proposed" by a made member (his sponsor, who is answerable for him), his name is circulated to all Five Families for objections (this list-circulation is what allowed the FBI to seize induction lists in the 1990s), and he is inducted in a ceremony. Historically a candidate had to have participated in a murder ("making your bones") to prove he was not an informant; this was relaxed as membership shrank.
- **Books closed 1957–1976**: after the Apalachin fiasco and at Carlo Gambino's insistence, the Commission closed membership for almost 20 years. In 1976 the Commission allowed a "limited opening" of 10 new members per family. This is why so many 1970s–80s stars (Gravano, made 1976) were inducted in that window.
- Promotions to capo are at the boss's discretion; a boss frequently breaks up and reassigns crews, "shelves" (deactivates) members, or demotes a capo to soldier.
- Introductions are formal: "a friend of ours" = made man, "a friend of mine" = associate. Only a made man can introduce a made man to another.

### The Commission and the settlement of disputes
Luciano created the Commission in 1931 after the Castellammarese War, to replace the "boss of bosses" with a board: the five New York bosses plus Chicago and Buffalo (later Detroit and Philadelphia sat at times). Its jobs: approve new bosses, approve the murder of bosses, arbitrate inter-family disputes over territory and rackets, and set policy (e.g., the drug ban, the 1957 book-closing). Sicily built its own Commissione in 1957–58 after a Palermo meeting attended by Joe Bonanno.

Dispute resolution is via the **sit-down**: a meeting between the ranks concerned, escalating upward. Soldier vs soldier is settled by their capos; capo vs capo by the administrations; family vs family by the bosses or the Commission. The loser generally pays, apologizes or hands over a racket; a sit-down is also where a "contract" on a member is approved or denied. A made man may only be killed with his own boss's approval; a boss may only be killed with Commission approval (Galante 1979 was Commission-approved; Castellano 1985 was not, and Gotti's failure to get formal approval was held against him by Gigante, who plotted to kill him). Attacking a made man without permission is a capital offense.

### Sicilian ranks
Sicilian Cosa Nostra uses cosca/famiglia with capofamiglia (elected by members), sottocapo, consigliere, capodecina (leads a decina of ~10 soldiers) and uomini d'onore. Three (sometimes more) contiguous families form a **mandamento**, headed by a **capomandamento** who sits on the **Provincial Commission**; a regional Commission (Cupola/Regione, formed in the 1970s) coordinates the provinces. Palermo city has 8 mandamenti and the province 7 more; Sicily today has roughly 94 families in 29 mandamenti, ~3,500–5,500 members. Sicilian families are territorial (a village, a city ward) rather than defined by rackets; the American families are defined by membership and overlap geographically.

## 2. Rules and culture

### Initiation
The American ceremony (Gravano's 1992 testimony): the candidate is driven around, brought to a room of made men, asked "do you know why you are here?", his trigger finger is pricked, blood dripped on a saint card, the card is set alight in his cupped hands while he swears to burn like the saint if he betrays the family. He is then told the rules: no drug dealing, no killing with bombs, no touching another member's wife or children, never raise your hand to another member, never speak to police, always come when called, the family comes before God and blood family. The Sicilian punciuta is the same in substance.

### Rules with teeth
- **Omertà**: silence toward the state, and not just about yourself. Testifying against anyone is death; cooperating members' relatives are historically not touched in the US (not so in Sicily, where Riina had children of pentiti killed, e.g., Giuseppe Di Matteo, 1996).
- **Drugs**: The Commission and several bosses (Gambino's "deal and die", Castellano's edict covering anyone made after 1962) banned narcotics because 20-year sentences produce informants. The ban was hypocritical and widely broken (Gotti's own crew, the Bonanno "Zips", the Lucchese). The Bonannos were effectively expelled from the Commission in the early 1980s after the Galante drug wars and the Brasco infiltration.
- **Wives and family**: no affairs with members' wives; members' families were off-limits in wars (broken in Sicily by the Corleonesi).
- **Cops and informants**: no member may be a cop or have a cop relative without disclosure; anyone who talks is killed (the Mafia Cops Eppolito and Caracappa were the inverse: cops working for Casso).
- **"Being on record"**: every associate must be "on record" with a specific made man, who claims him and is responsible for his conduct and debts. Two members claiming the same associate or racket produces a sit-down; an associate cannot switch sponsors without permission. Claims on businesses and people are the property system of the Mafia.
- **Kicking up / tribute**: no fixed universal percentage exists in the sources, but real examples: Michael Franzese sent 30% of his gasoline-tax proceeds to the Colombo administration weekly; the Concrete Club paid 2% of contract value to the Commission; the Colombos took 1.5–3 cents per gallon from Russian bootleggers; Gravano put Gotti's construction take alone at over $1M/year and his total at $5–12M/year. Typical folk rules reported by cooperators: an associate splits roughly half with his sponsor, a soldier gives his capo a fixed weekly/monthly "envelope" or a percentage (often described as 10–50% depending on whether the capo put up the capital), and capos pass a portion plus Christmas tribute to the boss. The boss's take comes almost entirely from these envelopes, from the unions and from industry-wide skims, not from personal crimes.
- **Earners vs muscle**: a "good earner" is protected and promoted even if disliked; "muscle" (hitters) get status but are the first killed when they become liabilities (DeMeo, killed 1983 on Castellano's order because he might flip).
- **Going to the mattresses**: in a war, soldiers move into safe apartments with mattresses on the floor, abandon routines and families' homes, and travel in groups.

## 3. Rackets and economics

- **Protection / extortion (pizzo)**: In Palermo the pizzo averages €457/month for retailers and €578 for hotels and restaurants; construction firms pay €2,000+; ranges run €50–100 for a street vendor to €10,000 for large firms. Investigators estimated €160M/year extorted in the Palermo region (2008) and ~10x that across Sicily; historically ~80% of Palermo shops paid. In the US the equivalent is the "street tax" (Chicago) and the "mob tax" on industries (Fulton Fish Market, carting).
- **Loansharking**: 2–5 "points" per week (1 point = 1%/week = 52% APR; 3 points = 156%); Valachi's customers paid 400–500% annualized; a 2010s Philadelphia case had rates to 264%. Missed vig rolls into principal. Capital typically comes from the capo, who lends to the soldier at 1 point who lends out at 3–5.
- **Gambling**: numbers (policy) with 600:1 payouts on 1000:1 odds, bookmaking with 10% vig ("juice") on losing bets, casinos and card games. The 2025 NBA poker indictments (Genovese, Bonanno, Gambino, Lucchese; $7M+ rigged) show it is still the core racket.
- **Labor racketeering**: control of union locals (Teamsters 560 Newark, Genovese/Provenzano; Teamsters 282 concrete trucking, Gambino; Cement Workers 6A, Colombo/Scopo; ILA waterfront, Genovese/Gambino; Laborers, HERE) yields: extortion of employers for "labor peace", no-show jobs for members and relatives, embezzlement of welfare funds, and pension-fund loans (the Teamsters Central States fund lent $62.7M to Allen Glick's Argent Corp. for the Stardust and Fremont).
- **Construction / the Concrete Club**: NYC contracts of $2–15M were allocated to a club of contractors chosen by the Commission; contractors paid 2% of contract value; Scopo collected, the four participating families split it. Bid-rigging via union control let the mob set who could pour concrete in Manhattan.
- **Garbage carting**: a "property rights" cartel in which each customer stop was owned by a carter and could be sold; the Five Families entered in 1956 when NYC forced 50,000 businesses to hire private carters; a $1.5B/year industry until the 1995 indictments and the 1996 Trade Waste Commission, after which prices fell sharply.
- **Fulton Fish Market**: Genovese control through Local 359 from Joseph "Socks" Lanza (1923–68) to Carmine Romano; Giuliani said fear had shrunk the market from ~$3B to ~$1B before the 1995 city takeover.
- **JFK airport / hijacking**: Lucchese and Gambino control of Teamsters Locals 295/851 gave inside information for cargo theft and truck hijacking; the 1978 Lufthansa heist ($5.875M) by Jimmy Burke's Lucchese-associated crew was followed by the murder of most participants to prevent talking.
- **Fuel-tax "daisy chain"**: 1980s Colombo (Franzese) and Russian (Balagula) operators collected state/federal gasoline excise (~30 cents/gallon) through shell wholesalers that vanished before remitting; Franzese claimed $5–8M/week, kicking 30% up.
- **Stock fraud**: pump-and-dump microcap schemes ran through mob-controlled brokerages in the 1990s; the SEC testified in 2000 that all Five Families were involved; Bonanno capo Frank Coppa is credited with pioneering the racket.
- **Narcotics**: the French Connection (Marseille labs, Turkish opium, ~1950s–72) supplied New York families; the Pizza Connection (1975–84) moved an estimated $1.65B of heroin via Sicilian "Zips" using pizzerias as fronts; the 1985–87 trial produced 18 convictions and was the longest US federal trial.
- **Las Vegas skim**: cash removed from count rooms before taxation at the Stardust, Fremont, Hacienda, Marina (Argent) and Tropicana, couriered to Chicago, Kansas City, Milwaukee and Cleveland; the Strawman cases (trials 1983, 1985–86) convicted Chicago boss Aiuppa, Cerone and others; total Argent skim estimates run to $7–15M.
- **Money laundering**: restaurants, social clubs, bars, vending, trucking, garment factories (Thomas Gambino's Garment District trucking) provide "legitimate" income and cash-mixing.
- **Incomes**: boss (Gotti) $5–12M/year per Gravano; star capo (Franzese) $5M/week at peak; ordinary soldiers vary from six figures to near-poverty. Pistone found Bonanno soldier Lefty Ruggiero chronically broke and in debt; most soldiers earn in the tens to low hundreds of thousands and depend on a legit job or no-show union job.

## 4. Violence

- **When murders were ordered**: informing or suspected informing (DeMeo 1983, Napolitano 1981 after Brasco, Willie Boy Johnson 1988), unsanctioned killing, stealing from the family, drug dealing in defiance of the boss (used as pretext), disrespect to the boss, and succession struggles. Murders of bosses (Masseria 1931, Maranzano 1931, Anastasia 1957, Galante 1979, Castellano 1985) were either Commission-approved or presented as fait accompli by a faction strong enough to be forgiven.
- **How hits were planned**: the boss gives the order through the underboss/capo (a buffer); a "crew" is assembled with shooters, drivers, a spotter and a "crash car"; the victim is lured by a trusted friend to a meeting (Castellano at Sparks Steak House; Gallo at Umberto's 1972); bodies are either left for message value or disappear (DeMeo's Gemini Lounge dismemberment).
- **Making your bones**: participation in a murder proved loyalty, filtered out undercover agents (the Brasco operation ended when Pistone was ordered to kill), and created mutual hostage-taking via shared guilt.
- **Wars**: Castellammarese War (Feb 1930–Apr 1931, ~60 dead), ended with Luciano betraying Masseria then killing Maranzano. Gallo–Profaci/Colombo wars: 1960–63 (~9 dead), 1971–72 (Colombo shot, Gallo killed), 1991–93 (Orena vs Persico, 12 dead). Banana War 1964–68 (Bonanno deposed by Commission). Sicily: First Mafia War 1962–63 ended by the Ciaculli car bomb killing 7 policemen; Second Mafia War 1981–83 in which the Corleonesi (Leggio, Riina, Provenzano) killed Bontate (Apr 23, 1981), Inzerillo (May 1981) and several hundred to 1,000 people, then purged even their own allies to take the Commission.
- **Consequences of unsanctioned violence**: a sit-down and death, or a settlement paid in rackets. Gotti survived because he pre-arranged support from the Lucchese and Colombo factions; Gigante still ordered his killing (the 1986 DeCicco car bomb was aimed at Gotti, breaking the "no bombs" rule).

## 5. Law enforcement and the decline

- **1957 Apalachin**: 60+ bosses detained; Hoover forced to admit the Mafia exists; Top Hoodlum Program with (then illegal) bugs.
- **1963 Valachi**: first member to testify publicly; led to the 1968 Title III Wiretap Act (legal, court-ordered bugs) and the 1970 Organized Crime Control Act which contained both **RICO** and the **Witness Security Program**.
- **RICO (1970, first big use ~1980s)**: made it a crime to run an "enterprise" through a "pattern of racketeering" (two predicate acts in 10 years), with 20 years per count, forfeiture, and civil trusteeships over unions. This let prosecutors convict a boss for the enterprise without proving he personally committed the predicates.
- **Undercover**: Pistone (1976–81) produced 200 indictments, 120 convictions, and a $500,000 contract on his life; the Bonannos lost their Commission seat.
- **Commission Trial (indicted Feb 1985, tried Sept–Nov 1986)**: Salerno, Corallo, Persico, Langella, Santoro, Furnari, Scopo, Indelicato convicted; seven got 100-year sentences (Jan 1987), largely on Title III recordings from Corallo's Jaguar and the Palma Boys club.
- **Pizza Connection trial (1985–87)**: 18 convictions, Badalamenti 45 years.
- **Maxi Trial (Palermo, Feb 1986–Dec 1987; Cassation Jan 30, 1992)**: 475 indicted, 338 convicted, 19 life sentences, 2,665 years total; first judicial confirmation that Cosa Nostra exists as a single organization, built on Buscetta's and Contorno's testimony.
- **Gotti**: after three acquittals (one via a fixed juror), the FBI bugged an apartment above the Ravenite Social Club in Nov 1989, where Gotti spoke freely and disparaged Gravano; Gravano flipped in 1991 and Gotti was convicted on all counts April 2, 1992. Gotti's insistence that capos visit the Ravenite weekly gave the FBI the whole family's org chart on video.
- **Cooperators**: Gravano (19 murders, 5 years), then underbosses (Vitale, Casso, DiLeonardo) and finally a sitting boss (Massino, 2005).
- **Mafia response**: Massino banned social clubs and phones, forbade members from speaking his name (touch the ear), and met only in trusted cars or on walks; Gigante feigned insanity for 30 years and used Salerno as a front boss; families stopped inducting, imposed stricter Italian-blood rules, and shrank their circle of knowledge.
- **The weakened modern Mafia**: still active in gambling, loansharking, construction and unions, but with membership around 100–250 per NY family, few murders (Frank Cali's 2019 killing was by a non-member), and constant leaks; indictments in 2024–25 still name all families.

## 6. Daily life and texture

- **Social clubs** (Ravenite, Palma Boys, Triangle Civic Association, Bergin Hunt and Fish): storefront clubs where members "check in", show respect, receive assignments, pay envelopes, play cards; also the FBI's main bugging target.
- **Walk-talks**: conversations conducted while walking, lips barely moving, to defeat bugs and lip-readers; the FBI countered with street cameras.
- **Messages** pass through the chain (boss to underboss to capo to soldier) so each level is a buffer; Provenzano used typed "pizzini" notes via couriers. Bosses never handle money or speak of crimes; capos deliver envelopes to the underboss.
- **Restaurants** (Sparks, Umberto's, Rao's) as neutral meeting grounds and fronts; funerals and weddings as sanctioned gatherings where all families see who ranks where (Bonanno's 1956 wedding; Castellano's unattended funeral as a snub).
- **Legit fronts**: every member has a "job" (salesman, trucking, construction, restaurants) for tax cover; no-show union jobs supply W-2 income.
- **Buffers**: a boss uses a front boss (Salerno), a "street boss" (modern Genovese practice) or a ruling panel so nothing traces to him; routine paranoia about phones, cars, and new associates.

## 7. Sicilian specifics

- **Mandamenti and the Cupola**: the Commission was formed 1957–58, dissolved after Ciaculli 1963, rebuilt as a triumvirate (Bontate, Badalamenti, Leggio) in 1970 and full Commission in the mid-1970s under Badalamenti then Michele Greco.
- **Politics and the "Third Level"**: Cosa Nostra delivered votes to Christian Democrat factions in exchange for contracts and judicial fixes; the pentito Mutolo described the "normal circuit" as Ignazio Salvo → Salvo Lima → Andreotti. Lima was murdered March 12, 1992 for failing to fix the Maxi Trial appeal. Andreotti was acquitted (1999); the 2003 appeal ruled he had associated with the Mafia until 1980 but the offense was time-barred.
- **Corleonesi takeover**: Leggio, Riina and Provenzano used heroin money, secret recruitment and alliances inside rival families to win the Second Mafia War, then ruled the Commission dictatorially.
- **1992**: after Cassation upheld the Maxi Trial (Jan 30, 1992), Riina ordered the Capaci bombing (May 23, Falcone, wife and three escorts) and Via D'Amelio (July 19, Borsellino and five officers). The state responded with Article 41-bis (harsh isolation regime extended to mafia prisoners in 1992), mass arrests and Riina's capture Jan 15, 1993; the 1993 bomb campaign in Rome, Florence and Milan followed.
- **Submersion**: Provenzano's strategy from 1993: no spectacular violence, quiet procurement fraud, coexistence with the state, communication by pizzini; he was captured 2006 after 43 years on the run, Messina Denaro in Jan 2023 after 30 years.

## Gameable mechanics (real dynamics)

1. **Tribute flows up, not commands**: associates pay their sponsoring soldier, soldiers pay their capo an envelope or share (real cases range 10–50%; Franzese paid 30%), capos pay the administration; a boss's whole income is tribute plus industry skims. Loyalty should track envelope size and regularity, not orders obeyed.
2. **Earner vs muscle**: high earners are protected and promoted; high-violence members gain fear but become liabilities and are the first killed when heat rises.
3. **Made-man status is a legal shield**: killing or striking a made man requires the boss's approval; killing a boss requires the Commission; an associate can be killed cheaply. Violating this triggers a sit-down and probable death.
4. **"On record" ownership**: every associate and every business/racket is claimed by one made man; overlapping claims force a sit-down and the loser pays or surrenders the asset.
5. **Sit-downs as the court**: disputes escalate rank by rank; outcomes are compensation, transfer of rackets, or an authorized contract. Most conflict should resolve here, with war as a rare failure state.
6. **The Commission as a cartel board**: approves bosses, arbitrates territory, sets bans (drugs 1950s–80s), and can expel a family (Bonannos 1980s). Losing your seat cuts you out of shared rackets like the Concrete Club.
7. **Books open/closed**: membership inflow is a policy lever; closed books (1957–76) starve growth but reduce infiltration; opening them (10 per family in 1976) accelerates growth and risk.
8. **Sponsor liability**: the member who proposes a recruit answers with his life if the recruit turns out to be an informant or agent (Napolitano and Mirra died for Brasco).
9. **Making your bones**: requiring a murder for membership screens out undercover agents but creates evidence and future cooperators; relaxing it increases recruitment and infiltration risk.
10. **Drug ban tradeoff**: narcotics are the highest-margin racket but carry the longest sentences and therefore the highest flip probability; bosses ban them to protect themselves while soldiers deal secretly.
11. **Loanshark math**: 2–5 points/week on principal that never amortizes; capital comes from the capo at ~1 point, so the soldier's spread is the profit; defaults convert to asset seizure (a bar, a truck, a union card) rather than cash.
12. **Pizzo / street tax scales with business size**: €50–100 for a vendor, €457 average for shops, €2,000+ for construction; compliance ~80% in Palermo but falls as anti-Mafia sentiment rises. Extortion revenue is stable but visibility invites prosecution.
13. **Union control multiplies everything**: owning a local yields labor-peace extortion, no-show jobs, welfare-fund theft, pension loans, and the power to run a cartel (Concrete Club 2% on $2–15M contracts). Unions are the highest-value capturable institution.
14. **Cartel "property rights"**: in carting, customers are owned and tradeable; margins come from overcharging but collapse when the state installs a regulator.
15. **Skims need insiders and couriers**: casino skims require a straw owner, a count-room insider and a courier chain; each link is a potential witness.
16. **Boss as buffer**: the boss never touches money or gives direct orders; every extra intermediary reduces conviction risk but slows and distorts commands and lets underlings skim.
17. **Fixed rituals are surveillance leaks**: mandatory weekly check-ins at a social club (Gotti) let the FBI map the whole family; walk-talks and no-phone policies (Massino) reduce evidence but reduce coordination.
18. **Every arrest is a flip roll**: probability of cooperation rises with sentence length (RICO 20 years/count), murder exposure, and hearing yourself disparaged on tape (Gravano); a flipped underboss can take down the entire administration.
19. **RICO changes the target**: pre-1980, prosecuting a predicate crime removed one soldier; post-RICO, proving an "enterprise" removes the hierarchy and forfeits assets. Law-enforcement pressure should switch from per-crime to per-organization mid-timeline.
20. **Wiretaps as the main evidence source**: bugs in clubs, cars and homes produced the Commission and Gotti convictions; counter-surveillance spending directly lowers conviction odds.
21. **Wars are succession failures**: every major war started over who is boss, not over territory; wars kill ~10–60 in the US and hundreds in Sicily and always end with the state stronger.
22. **Going to the mattresses**: war state means income drops (no one collects), soldiers live in safe houses, and civilians are avoided; prolonging war bleeds tribute.
23. **Unsanctioned hits can succeed if the coalition is big enough**: Gotti killed Castellano without Commission approval and survived by prearranging Lucchese and Colombo support; power can be seized, but the grudge persists.
24. **Sicilian territory vs American membership**: Sicilian families own turf and every activity within it; American families own people and rackets that overlap in the same city. Two different map models.
25. **Politics as a racket in Sicily**: votes delivered to a faction buy contracts and judicial fixes; when the fix fails (Lima 1992) the politician is killed, which brings a state crackdown.
26. **Visibility is a slider**: Riina's terror strategy maximized deterrence and triggered maximum repression; Provenzano's submersion minimized violence, restored income and survived 13 more years. Public violence should raise heat far faster than it raises income.
27. **41-bis / isolation regimes**: imprisoned bosses lose command when communication is cut; before 41-bis, bosses ruled from prison (Persico) so imprisonment was not removal.
28. **Legit fronts as laundering and cover**: restaurants, trucking, garment shops and no-show jobs convert cash into taxable income and give members an alibi occupation; too little legit income triggers tax cases.
29. **Shrinking pool**: stricter blood rules, closed books and cooperators shrink each family from ~250 to ~100–200; a late-game family is income-rich in gambling but thin in soldiers and constantly leaking.
30. **Introductions and reputation**: "friend of ours" vs "friend of mine" governs who may deal with whom; a made man's word binds his family, so reputation is transferable and damage to it is punishable.

## Open questions from the research
- Exact tribute percentages are not codified anywhere; the best documented data points are Franzese's 30%, the Concrete Club's 2%, and the 1.5–3 cents/gallon gasoline tribute. A designer will need to pick a schedule (e.g., associate 50% to soldier, soldier 10–25% to capo plus fixed monthly envelope, capo 10–20% plus Christmas tribute to boss) and treat it as plausible rather than sourced.
- Soldier-level income is anecdotal; the Gravano and Massino trial transcripts and the 1986 President's Commission on Organized Crime report are the next sources.
- Italian-language DIA semi-annual reports would give current Sicilian family counts and pizzo estimates more precisely.
